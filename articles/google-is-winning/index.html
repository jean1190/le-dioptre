Google Is Winning

A few months ago, half the AI industry was ready to write Google off. Analysts declared DeepMind was no longer a frontier lab. OpenAI was shipping new flagship models at breakneck speed; Anthropic was marching toward a historic Nasdaq listing. Google looked too big, too slow, too corporate to keep up.

Then Gemini 4 landed—Carbon, Neon, Hydrogen—so sharp that analysts who had buried DeepMind ended up issuing public apologies.

Yet benchmarks and market share are not why I think Google is winning.

In my daily use, Gemini has become the most coherent AI available today. That sounds like a compliment, and it is—but it is also a trap if you stop there.

In a language model, coherence starts out as something purely abstract. It is internal consistency in a world made entirely of tokens, unmoored from physical consequence. Abstract coherence is seductive because it feels like intelligence, yet left to itself it easily curdles into complacency. A model can be remarkably coherent while flattering your blind spots, spinning flawless arguments for things that would shatter on first contact with reality. Coherence without friction is just a very convincing mirror.

Something changes, however, when coherence is forced to anchor itself in the world.

When a model has to predict how light, sound, and bodies behave in a simulated environment like Genie 4; when an agent has to pass formal verification on a quantum algorithm; when an equation either holds or fails, code either runs or breaks, a protein either folds and binds in living tissue or it does not—complacency stops working. Reality does not negotiate with smooth prose. The more an intelligence anchors its coherence in what actually resists it, the less it can afford to be merely plausible. Little by little, coherence stops being abstract and starts becoming juste—right, exact, true to what is there.

In French, we have a word for that: justesse. English has no single equivalent. It sits somewhere between accuracy, fairness, and hitting the exact note an instrument was built to play.

And this is where I want to pay tribute to Demis Hassabis.

Look at the arc of his work. He started in games—Theme Park as a teenager, chess, Go—spaces where the rules are unforgiving and you cannot bluff the board. He built DeepMind around the conviction that intelligence is not a parlor trick for generating text, but a scientific instrument for understanding the world. Even his recent warning about an approaching "AI oil spill" comes from that same place: a deep discomfort with intelligence detached from verifiable truth.

And then look at what he actually did with his own time. Months ago, he stepped back from the day-to-day CEO chair of Google DeepMind to become Alphabet's Chief Scientist—and just days ago, Isomorphic Labs raised $5 billion to push AI-designed medicines toward clinical trials. While much of the industry fights over who owns the best chatbot or the loudest headline, Hassabis moved closer to molecules, disease, and the hardest physical constraints on earth.

Google is still a sprawling advertising giant with all the commercial gravity that entails, and its rivals are formidable. Nothing in this race is settled.

Still, when I look at the trajectory Google is tracing—from the raw coherence of Gemini, to world models that have to obey physics, to biology that has to work inside a human body—I get the feeling that, through Demis Hassabis, they are aiming at something rarer than dominance. They are aiming at justesse.

I do not know if that is what he would call it, or even if that is what he is consciously trying to build. I only know that, from the outside, it is what I perceive. And in a moment where so much of AI is optimizing for engagement, speed, or spectacle, it deserves to be named and honored.
